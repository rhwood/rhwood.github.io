## February

Ugh.

During a cold snap late January Denis, my father, passed out and slid out of his chair. He only bothered to tell anyone three or four days later and only bothered to tell his kids by forwarded an already forwarded email to two far remote also elderly friends of his.

He lives alone and burned most of his friendships thirty years ago but we managed to get his pilates instructor to check in on him the evening he notified us. She didn’t manage to get him to care that evening -- he refused to consider it -- but did manage to get him to agree to let her take him to urgent care the next day.

She didn’t. She took him straight to the Emergency Room at WakeMed. Probably avoided a lot of hassle, but they were packed such that although he was admitted immediately, he was in a hallway for six or seven hours before they were able to get him into a room, but he’d already had some specialized care by the time we made into Raleigh (it’s a five hour drive at best and we left late because I had a Coronary CT Angiography procedure that morning). At something like 10 o’clock that night, the ER doctors installed a drain in his abdomen (Chrysta and I had left him sleeping by then so we didn’t worthlessly collapse ourselves).

Turns out he’d ruptured his appendix and didn’t feel it until the trauma knocked him out entirely. His cleaning lady had been telling him he looks to be in pain and to go see a doctor, but he’s been blowing her off. I suspect he had normalized the pain from other injuries we didn’t know about and he didn’t recall and from bad habits he’d built up over the years.

We’d hear terms like *gangrene* and *sepsis* over the next few days as he recovered in intensive care enough for them to begin to talk to him about his future.

He spent about two weeks in the hospital, moving out of intensive care after about five days. We meanwhile spent the time getting my brother’s condominium that he rents out on Airbnb ready to bring my father into it because my father’s place was basically uninhabitable in his condition.

One of Denis’s medical issues stems from a back injury he ignored sometime in the mid-to-late 2010s until we brought his attention to atrophy in his right leg. He eventually went to see some doctors who sent him to physical therapy. The therapist he worked with (for months if not more than a year) eventually moved to California. I don’t think to get away from Denis, but sometimes I like to think so. I suspect Denis didn’t do anything the therapist asked him to do outside their sessions together. In any event, when the therapist ceased practice in Raleigh, Denis refused to find another therapist because (he said) he was afraid he would not like him as much as the old therapist.

He also began using a cane that a friend of his had been carrying around in the back of his pick up truck. No consideration given to size -- it turned out to be about three inches too tall for Denis -- and no consideration given to how to use a cane -- Denis used it like a crutch, on the wrong side.

So leaving the hospital and through the following surgeries, Denis is using a walker and has lost a lot of muscle in his abdomen; he had been relying on abdominal strength to correct for all the other mistakes he was making and couldn’t.

At some point this month, the bulb to drain his abdomen gets removed -- later than expected because of the shear amount of liquid draining -- and we schedule an appendectomy for early March.

A follow up appointment with his primary doctor reveals that Denis hasn’t been sharing information with her. Nothing. Not even willing to consider discussing his life with her -- what later turn out to be expected side effects of long term use of a prescribed drug he’s not been reporting. Not because he’s trying to hide them, but because he’s -- I don’t know -- so completely able to compartmentalize things that he’s always “fine.” Can’t feel his feet and suffering from neuropathy but will only tell his doctor he’s “fine.”

## March

Meanwhile, every indication is Denis suffers from near constant atrial fibrillation. Even the home health people hear it in stethoscopes.

Denis’s appendectomy is pretty much as expected but he stays a night (or was it two) in the hospital before they are comfortable discharging him to our care.

WakeMed tries to control Denis’s AFIB with an electrical cardioversion (the least invasive AFIB treatment).

I think there may have been two rounds of medication changes to deal with the mess that Denis’s medications have become; I think the mess is due to Denis’s non-communicativeness about his medications.

At some point we learn that Denis has fallen before although he refuses to acknowledge it; he claims he’s “tumbled,” not fallen and we learn that he’d been using his old cane to grab or pull at things instead of, oh I don’t know, supporting himself with it. We get him a correctly sized collapsable cane that can be stowed in a walker (and that, because it pulls apart, can’t be used to grab things).

We’ve rearranged his house, dismantling his dining room and bringing his bed down into the former dining room. A friend, a widow, has loaned us her husband’s bath chair that lowers into a bath, and we’ve gotten the floor of his downstairs bathroom redone. I jury rig a shower handle onto his bathtub, an old clawfoot one as he won’t countenance a shower, and at least the occupational therapist approves of that piece of work.

## April

The first electrical cardioversion didn’t hold for more than a week, so it’s clear more extreme measures will be needed to control the AFIB.

A second cardioversion is performed early in the month and an ablation is scheduled for late in the month.

Meanwhile Denis tries using a cane (a new one we bought him) and falls twice in one week. He has been walking more, both with an upright walker and the standard little one you see in hospitals. He doesn’t like the upright walker because while it provides support, he can’t use it like a passive crutch to carry him, while he can lean heavily on the small walker. The first fall we think is on leaves right beside the car, but the second is on a clear dry sidewalk. It appears he’s reaching too far and expecting to be able to fall onto the cane to move and so we stop attempting to use it.

We get Denis back home and stay in the Airbnb, visiting him daily and taking him to various appointments.

## May

We are finally able to leave; we have to leave; my employer has been understanding, but only to a point, and Chrysta is frazzled and feeling done in.

This is only the first half of the year and we will be back far sooner than expected.
